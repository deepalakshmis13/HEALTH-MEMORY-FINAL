Title of the Project

Health Memory — An AI-Agent-Powered Persistent Health Memory Layer for Elderly Patients, Guardians, Doctors, Caregivers, Old-Age Homes and Pharmacists.

02

Problem Statement

An elderly patient's health information is scattered across doctors, pharmacies, caregivers and hospitals that never communicate with one another — so a changed dose, an illegibly handwritten prescription or a known allergy is lost at exactly the moment it matters most, leading to avoidable medication errors, repeated tests and unsafe emergency care.

India has over 149 million citizens aged 60 and above. A typical elderly patient consults a specialist at one hospital, a physician at a neighbourhood clinic, buys medicines from a pharmacy that keeps no record, and is cared for daily by a family member or facility attendant who has never met either doctor. The information exists; the connection between the people holding it does not.

03

Solution Proposed

A working web platform on which an elderly patient or their guardian builds a single, permanent, consent-controlled health memory from three kinds of everyday input, and then decides — scope by scope — which of the people caring for them may read which part of it. Four role-specific AI assistants read that memory under those permissions and answer with visible evidence.

The core pipeline

01

Add

Type it, say it, or photograph the document — including handwritten prescriptions.

02

Understand

OCR, handwriting recognition and medical entity extraction produce structured facts, each with a confidence score.

03

Verify

Low-confidence and clinically important fields are routed to a licensed pharmacist for human confirmation.

04

Connect

Authorised doctors, caregivers and pharmacists retrieve only what consent permits, with the source shown.

Key features

FeatureWhat it does
Three-mode captureText, voice (browser speech recognition with a typed fallback) and scan/upload. All three enter the same pipeline and keep their original wording.
Handwriting OCRA dedicated recognition engine for handwritten prescriptions, with calibrated confidence and approximate drug-name matching against a medical lexicon.
Confidence-gated verificationEvery extracted field is scored. A field reaches a pharmacist only if it is below the trust threshold and clinically important.
Consent Control CenterThe patient grants or revokes seven information scopes across five recipient types. Every change is logged and takes effect immediately.
Doctor routingA scanned prescription that names a prescriber is automatically associated with that doctor — without ever fabricating a doctor account.
Four role dashboardsPatient/Guardian, Doctor, Caregiver & Old-Age Home, Pharmacist — one record, four working surfaces.
Shift-based care workflowMedication rounds, care tasks, observations and an AI-assisted handover for individual caregivers and facility rotas.
One-click visit summaryA longitudinal, evidence-backed draft the doctor reviews and edits before it is saved — never auto-committed.
Emergency Health CardBlood group, allergies, critical conditions, verified medications, emergency contact and primary doctor — readable at arm's length and printable.
Four role-specific agentsSeparate assistants for each role, each answering in the appropriate register, each showing its sources.

Mechanisms that make it trustworthy

  • Provenance on every fact. Each record permanently carries its source, author, confidence and verification status. Patient-reported information is never presented as a doctor-confirmed diagnosis.
  • Selective human-in-the-loop. A doctor's name misread at 66% does not interrupt anyone; a dose misread at 66% does. The routing rule is written once and applies everywhere.
  • Consent enforced before retrieval. Unauthorised records are removed from the candidate set before relevance is ever scored — the language model is never handed something it is asked to ignore.
  • Non-destructive correction. A pharmacist's correction is stored beside the original reading and its score, never on top of it.

Current status

This is not a concept. A complete working prototype exists: a full backend, four functioning dashboards, the OCR and verification pipeline, consent-aware retrieval and all four assistants, verified by an automated end-to-end test suite.

Safety and Efficiency Analysis

Scope note. The materials this system handles are medication, medical documents and clinical information. The analysis below addresses safety and efficiency in the handling of those materials and in the daily care tasks built around them.

Safety — five mechanisms, each enforced in the backend

RiskMechanismWhy it holds
A misread dose or drug name is administered Confidence-gated pharmacist verification, then a hard administration interlock A caregiver attempting to record an unverified medication as administered receives a server-side refusal, not a dismissible warning. The rule cannot be bypassed from the interface.
An AI answer is mistaken for a clinical fact Provenance carried inline into every retrieved record; every AI output labelled and shown with its sources The assistant physically cannot present an unverified reading as confirmed without contradicting the labelled text in front of it. Every answer exposes a "why am I seeing this?" evidence panel.
An AI summary silently becomes the medical record Draft-and-review: generation and acceptance are separate, audited steps A visit summary is stored as a draft until a clinician edits and saves it; only then is it recorded, and it is stamped as a doctor-reviewed AI draft.
Information reaches someone it should not Patient-level authorization plus consent filtering applied before retrieval A signed-in doctor with no care relationship is refused and the refusal is logged. Unauthorised records never enter the candidate set, so no prompt instruction is relied upon.
An error cannot be traced after the fact Non-destructive correction plus an append-only audit log of 21 action types The original reading, its confidence, the correction, the pharmacist and the timestamp all persist together. Every access, retrieval, consent change and verification is recorded.

What the system refuses to do

  • It does not invent a medication, dose, diagnosis, date, result or doctor.
  • It does not fabricate a doctor account — an unregistered prescriber is recorded as an external provider so the source survives.
  • It does not prescribe, alter treatment, or present OCR uncertainty as fact.
  • It does not list unverified medication on the Emergency Health Card.

Efficiency — where the time goes today, and where it goes after

TaskCurrent handlingWith the platform
Retrieving a patient's historySearching a paper file or asking the family to recall it; often re-taken from scratch.A longitudinal timeline retrieved in seconds, with the source of every entry.
Reading a handwritten prescriptionPharmacist telephones the prescriber, or interprets it under time pressure.Only genuinely uncertain, clinically important fields are queued — with the original page, the reading and its score side by side.
Shift handoverVerbal, or a handwritten register; detail is lost between shifts.A structured handover assembled from the shift's actual medication records, observations and pending tasks, reviewed before saving.
Medication roundsA paper chart per resident; missed doses noticed late or not at all.Rounds generated automatically for the shift window; missed doses surface immediately as alerts.
Preparing for a consultationReconstructing what changed since the last visit from whatever the patient brought.A one-click draft covering history, medications, changes, labs, caregiver observations, alerts and unresolved issues.
Emergency responseSearching for records, or proceeding without them.A single card with blood group, allergies, critical conditions and verified medications — printable and readable at arm's length.

Efficiency by design, not by exhortation

  • Selective escalation. Because only clinically important low-confidence fields queue, a pharmacist reviews a handful of items per document rather than every field — the difference between a workable queue and an ignored one.
  • Capture where the patient already is. Voice input and phone-camera capture mean no data-entry step is added to anyone's day.
  • Write once, read everywhere. A caregiver's observation is simultaneously the doctor's context and the next shift's handover material. No duplicate recording.
  • Zero external dependency. The system runs fully offline with no API key, so a connectivity failure in a facility does not stop care.

Organisational scaling

  • One facility → a chain. The facility, staff, shift and assignment model already exists; multi-facility isolation is a tenancy boundary on top of it.
  • Institutional → district. Consent scopes and audit logging are the preconditions for a public-health deployment where data crosses institutional lines.
  • National integration. The layer is designed to federate with India's ABDM / ABHA health ID rather than compete with it — a patient's ABHA number becomes their identity, and this becomes the consent and provenance layer on top.

Adaptation to adjacent environments

The architecture is not specific to geriatrics. The same memory layer — provenance, confidence, verification, consent — transfers directly to:

Post-operative care

Recovery at home with a rotating set of visiting nurses, where handover and medication tracking are the entire problem.

Chronic disease

Diabetes and hypertension management across a physician, a specialist and a pharmacy that share no record.

Rural primary care

A PHC where the visiting doctor changes and offline operation is mandatory, not optional.

Palliative care

Where family, nursing staff and a palliative team must stay aligned on a rapidly changing plan.

Maternal health

Antenatal records that must follow the mother across ANM, PHC and hospital.

Disability support

Long-term care where a support worker's daily observation is clinically meaningful.

Innovation Advantage

Four things that do not exist together in any current product

1 · Handwriting is a first-class input, not a failure case. Most document pipelines treat a handwritten prescription as a bad scan to be rejected. Here it has its own recognition engine, its own confidence calibration and its own downstream consequence. Because OCR of cursive script returns fragments rather than clean words, drug extraction matches approximately against a medical lexicon and lowers its own confidence by the similarity gap — reporting "probably Amlodipine, but only 54% certain" rather than silently asserting it. The raw reading is preserved for the human reviewer.
2 · Uncertainty is routed to a human, selectively. A verification task is created only when a field is both below the trust threshold and clinically important. A doctor's name read at 66% confidence does not interrupt anyone. A dose read at 66% does. This is the difference between a verification queue a pharmacist actually works and one they learn to ignore — and it is what makes human-in-the-loop economically viable at scale.
3 · Consent is a retrieval filter, not an interface toggle. Unauthorised records are removed from the candidate set before relevance is scored, so the AI is never handed information it is merely instructed to ignore. Revoking a scope removes those records from the timeline, the summary and the assistant in the same instant. Most systems enforce permissions at the screen; this one enforces them at the data.
4 · Provenance survives every transformation. A pharmacist correcting a misread drug name does not overwrite anything. The original OCR string, its confidence, the corrected value, the pharmacist's identity and the timestamp all persist together and remain visible on the record months later. Trust is a property of the data, not a claim in the marketing.

Comparison with existing approaches

Existing approach What it does well Where it leaves the elderly patient
Hospital EHR / HIS Comprehensive within one institution; clinically mature. The record belongs to the institution. An elderly patient's care does not happen inside one institution, so the record fragments the moment they leave.
Personal health record apps Patient-owned; simple document storage. A folder of photographs. No structure, no verification, no way for a doctor to receive only the relevant part, no trust model.
ABDM / ABHA health ID National identity and consent framework; the right foundation. Provides linkage, not comprehension. It does not read a handwritten prescription, score it, or route uncertainty to a pharmacist. This platform is designed to sit on top of it, not against it.
Medical chatbots / LLM assistants Fluent natural-language interaction. Answer from a model, not from a verified record. No provenance, no consent boundary, no human verification — and they fail unsafely rather than visibly.
Old-age home management software Rosters, billing, basic medication charts. Operational, not clinical. No connection to the patient's doctors, no document understanding, no shared memory beyond the facility walls.
The defensible position. Competitors are strong on either connectivity (health IDs, EHRs) or comprehension (document AI, LLM assistants). This project is built at the intersection, and adds the piece both are missing: a calibrated, auditable account of how much each fact should be trusted, with a human in the loop exactly where — and only where — it matters.
Stated honestly. The prototype uses a pilot-scale drug lexicon and reasoned rather than corpus-fitted thresholds, and does not yet encrypt data at rest. These are named in the roadmap as prerequisites, not omitted from it.

The moment that changes everything

Stage 3 is where the project earns its place. A single handwritten prescription is the point at which an elderly patient's care most often goes wrong — and it is the point every existing product either ignores or fails at silently.

Before — silent failure

The slip says something like "Amlodipine 5 mg". The pharmacist cannot be sure. There is no time to call, so the most likely reading is dispensed.

The caregiver administers what is in the box. The facility register still shows the old dose. Nobody records that anyone was ever uncertain.

If harm follows, there is no trace of where the error entered.

→
After — visible, resolved, recorded

The page is photographed. The handwriting engine reads it and scores each field. "Amlodipine" comes back at 54% — below the trust threshold, and clinically important.

A high-priority task reaches a pharmacist with the original page beside the reading. She confirms it. The caregiver cannot administer it until she has.

The record permanently shows: read at 54%, confirmed by a named pharmacist, on a given date.
