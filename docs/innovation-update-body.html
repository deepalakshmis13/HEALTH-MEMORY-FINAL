01

The original prototype, and exactly what is being added

What was submitted at Stage 1

The prototype is a persistent health-memory layer for elderly patients. An elderly patient's medical history is normally scattered across paper prescriptions, several hospitals, a caregiver's recollection and a family member's phone. The prototype gives that history one home that never forgets and never loses provenance.

Information enters through three routes — typed text, spoken entry, or a photographed document. Scanned records pass through a handwriting-aware OCR stage that scores every extracted field. Fields below the confidence threshold 0.85 that are also clinically important (drug name, dose, frequency, allergy, critical instruction) are never written silently into the record: they are routed to a human verification queue. Everything stored carries who said it, when, from what source, at what confidence, and under which consent scope. Four role dashboards — Patient/Guardian, Doctor, Caregiver/Old Age Home, and the verification role — read the same memory through a consent-aware retrieval pipeline, each with a role-specific assistant that answers only from records that role is permitted to see.

What is new since that application

Three additions, built directly onto the existing codebase rather than as a new project. Together they address the gap between a system that works and a system an elderly Tamil-speaking patient in Chennai can actually use and trust.

NEW · A

Bilingual Tamil–English interface

Every interface string in both languages, switchable anywhere including before login.

NEW · B

Tamil and mixed-speech voice intake

Speak Tamil, English, or both in one sentence; an assisted English reading is derived.

NEW · C

Patient-controlled care team

The patient, not the doctor, decides which doctors may see the record.

NEW · D

Document data layer

The store moved from a single-file relational database to MongoDB collections.

A · The bilingual layer

926 interface strings are now available in English and தமிழ். The switch sits in the dashboard top bar, the public navigation and both authentication screens, so a patient or a guardian chooses the language before they have to read anything. Dates and relative times follow the choice. Clinical content — medication names, memory-event text, OCR output, AI-generated drafts, anything a clinician wrote — is deliberately never translated; only interface chrome is.

B · Voice that accepts how people actually speak

The voice diary now offers three speaking modes: English, தமிழ், and தமிழ் + English. A new language service detects the script mix of any patient entry — typed or spoken — and derives a plain-English assisted reading so that extraction can work and an English-reading clinician can follow the entry:

Input (Tamil speech): எனக்கு இரண்டு நாளாக தலை வலிக்குது
Assisted reading: I have had a headache for two days.
Stored as: the patient's original Tamil wording, event type SYMPTOM, trust level Patient Reported, confidence capped at 0.88 — not a diagnosis.

The reading is produced by a reviewable phrase table, not a translation service. Every mapping is a fixed, inspectable pair covering the vocabulary elderly patients actually use — symptoms, body parts, durations, medicine and visit language. Anything outside the table stays in the patient's own words rather than being guessed at.

C · Consent extended from data to relationship

Previously a doctor named in an uploaded prescription was linked to the patient automatically. That is convenient and wrong: it makes the record's reach a side effect of paperwork. Now the patient owns the relationship. They search a directory, add a doctor, hold several at once, nominate a primary, and remove any of them. Adding a doctor is what places the patient on that doctor's dashboard; removing takes them off it. A doctor named in a scanned record is suggested to the patient and receives nothing until the patient accepts. There is no code path anywhere in the backend by which a doctor can add, remove or alter a care relationship — including their own.

Removal withdraws access; it never deletes history. The relationship is marked removed and retained. Every memory event, prescription, medication, consultation, lab result, diagnosis, hospital visit and document that recorded that doctor still points at them, and the doctor remains listed as a past doctor with a count of the records they contributed — so the patient can see what that clinician gave them, and add them back later.

D · Supporting change: document data layer and role generalisation

The store moved from a single-file relational database to MongoDB, with the patient–doctor relationship modelled as a join collection that supports many doctors per patient and many patients per doctor, updated live as the patient edits their care team. The verification role was also generalised from Pharmacist to Reviewer, since the function is confirming low-confidence extracted data — work a pharmacist, a nurse or a clinical records officer can each do — while keeping that dashboard's behaviour and appearance identical.

02

How this improves the work, and why the approach is smarter and cheaper

What the update changes about the product

Stage 1 proved the record could be trusted. This stage makes it reachable. A 72-year-old in Chennai who is more fluent speaking Tamil than reading English could previously have a perfect health memory built about them by their guardian; they can now build it themselves, by speaking one sentence in the language they think in. And the person whose body the record describes now decides who reads it.

Against what exists today

Approach in the marketWhere it stopsWhat this project does instead
Hospital EMR / HIS Organised around one institution's episodes. The record does not follow the patient between hospitals, and the caregiver and family are not users at all. Organised around the patient's lifetime, with caregiver and guardian as first-class roles.
National health-ID record linkage Solves discovery and exchange of documents. It does not turn scattered documents into a usable memory, and it does not help someone enter what happened in their own words. Complements it: an intake and memory layer, with vernacular entry and provenance on every fact.
Consumer health apps Transactional — booking, ordering, teleconsult. History is a by-product, largely English, and the patient rarely controls onward visibility. History is the product; language and relationship control are the interface.
Cloud translation / LLM for vernacular support Per-call cost that scales with every elderly user; protected health information leaves the deployment; a generative translator can silently produce a clinical claim the patient never made. A fixed, auditable phrase table plus an English-keyed dictionary. No per-call cost, no network dependency, no PHI egress, and it cannot invent a symptom.

Why the engineering choices are the affordable ones

  • Translation as data, not as a service. The dictionary is keyed by the English source string itself, so there are no invented key names to maintain and a missing entry renders in English rather than as a blank or a raw key. A translation gap degrades to readable English instead of a broken screen — which is what makes it safe for a vocabulary maintained by clinicians and volunteers rather than by a vendor.
  • Determinism where safety is at stake. The same property that makes a phrase table less fluent than a neural translator is what makes it acceptable here: its output is reviewable in advance, identical every time, and incapable of adding meaning. The reading is explicitly an aid — the stored truth is the patient's own words, the confidence is capped, and the entry enters the same human-review workflow as any other patient statement.
  • No paid inference in the critical path. OCR runs locally, retrieval uses locally-computed embeddings, and the assistants fall back to a deterministic grounded provider when no model key is configured. The system is fully functional offline. Recurring cost is a small virtual machine and a database — not a per-request bill that grows with every patient served.
  • A migration that cost almost nothing to verify. Moving to MongoDB by rewriting all 143 query sites would have put the clinical logic — confidence routing, consent filtering, retrieval ranking — back on the table. Replacing the mapper underneath them instead left every service, route and agent module byte-identical, so the behaviour was not merely re-tested but structurally unchanged.

The biggest insight

The hard problem in vernacular healthcare software is not translating text. It is deciding what must not be translated, and being honest about how good the translation is.

Two findings drove that conclusion. First, the boundary between interface and clinical content turned out to be the real design work: a drug name, an OCR reading or a doctor's note rendered into Tamil is no longer evidence of what was written, so the system now treats clinical content as data that is displayed verbatim in either language, and translates only the chrome around it.

Second, and less comfortably: word-by-word mapping between Tamil and English produces grammatical nonsense on free-form sentences, because Tamil is subject–object–verb and English is subject–verb–object. The high-frequency clinical pattern renders perfectly; an unconstrained sentence does not. The honest output of the current system, shown unedited:

Constrained clinical pattern — works

எனக்கு இரண்டு நாளாக தலை வலிக்குது

→ "I have had a headache for two days."

Free sentence — degrades visibly

நேற்று டாக்டர் Arun Kumar-ஐ பார்த்தேன், அவர் என் blood pressure மாத்திரை மாற்றினார்

→ "Yesterday doctor Arun Kumar saw, he my blood pressure tablet changed."

That second output is understandable but clumsy, and the design response matters more than the clumsiness: rather than hide it behind a fluent generative translator that might smooth the sentence into a claim the patient did not make, the reading is labelled as assisted, its confidence is capped, and the patient's original wording remains the stored record. A clinician reading it can see both. Fluency was traded for the guarantee that nothing was invented.

The unexpected hurdle

Internationalisation turned out to be a layout problem as much as a language problem. Tamil labels run considerably longer than their English equivalents, and the public navigation — which fits comfortably in English at 1440 px — overflowed as soon as Tamil was selected. The fix was a breakpoint scoped to the active language, collapsing the link row into the existing menu earlier in Tamil only, leaving the English rendering untouched. The general lesson: a bilingual retrofit needs a visual regression pass in both languages at every breakpoint, not just a string audit.

A second hurdle was the database migration. The obvious route — rewriting every query to the new driver — would have been idiomatic and risky, touching the confidence-routing and consent-filtering logic that the whole safety argument rests on. Replacing the mapper beneath those queries instead was the smaller and far safer change, and the full suite passed unchanged afterwards.
